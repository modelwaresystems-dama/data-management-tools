0 Purpose — Know Your Value frame

Why the model exists before what it contains.

The whole system rests on one definition: Value = useful change for someone else. Effort ≠ value — if nothing changes, no value was created, regardless of work done. The gap between work done and value seen is the Visibility Gap. The CA&I exists to close it: it reads what an employer's work actually requires, measures where a person or team is against that, and drives the next useful change. When a person's role shifts, their value is re-based (the old scorecard no longer applies) and they Renegotiate, Redesign, or Relocate — the 3Rs.

Structurally the model is a chain: Demand (what the work requires) → Capability (what people hold) → Evidence (what they produced) → Assessment (how far from the target) → Improvement (develop or compose) → and back to demand. It runs continuously, which is the Continuous in CA&I.

1 The Demand Side — DMBOK what the work requires

Knowledge Area → Activity (Best Practice) → Deliverable.

Demand is expressed in DMBOK (aligned to the 2024 Revised Edition). A Knowledge Area (e.g. Data Governance) contains Activities — the Best Practices — each sitting in a phase: Planning, Development, Operations, Control. Every activity produces one or more Deliverables, and each deliverable carries a required mastery. The Data Governance KA, fully mapped, has 14 activities across Planning (9), Operations (4) and Control (1).

DAMA's Knowledge Areas group into four bands: Oversight (Data Governance, DM Maturity Assessment, DM Organisation & Roles, DM Organisational Change, Data Handling Ethics), Outcome (Data Value Realisation), Lifecycle (Architecture, Modelling & Design, Storage & Operations, Integration, Reference & Master, Warehousing & BI, Big Data, Document & Content) and Foundation (Data Security, Metadata).

Each activity is described by the DMBOK context diagram — the DAMA "pitchfork": Definition, Goals & Principles, Inputs/Suppliers, Activities, Deliverables, Consumers, Participants, Tools, Techniques, Metrics. The Deliverable is the hinge between demand and evidence (§7).

The hinge: a Best Practice doesn't require a job title — it requires a set of KSCAs (§2). That is what makes the model role-agnostic and assessable.

2 The KSCA Framework what capability is

Knowledge · Skills · Competencies · Attitudes — the capability an activity requires.

Each Best Practice resolves to four kinds of capability:

KKnowledge — what you must know SSkills — what you can do (SFIA) CCompetencies — the responsibility you carry (SFIA generic attributes) AAttitudes — how you're wired (disposition)

Knowledge spans several bodies of knowledge, not just DMBOK: DMBOK, BizBOK (business architecture), BABOK (business analysis), PMBOK (project/programme), Change Management, Records Management and Data Science. Skills are SFIA skills (§4). Competencies are the SFIA generic attributes at a level of responsibility. Attitudes are Gregorc Thinking Styles and CliftonStrengths (§5).

The crucial split: K, S and C are developable and held by an individual — you learn them and level up (§3), and a team pools them. Attitudes are dispositional — natural wiring that doesn't change with training — so they are composed across a team, not trained into one person (§6, §9).

3 The Mastery Scale developable axis

How deep — the named scale K/S/C are measured on.

Knowledge, Skills and Competencies are measured on a named, six-point scale (plus an "incomplete" state). It runs from no capability to the ability to set direction:

LevelNameKey characteristic
M-1IncompleteAssessment incomplete
M0UnawareAbsence of capability
M1AwareSuccess depends on team members' support
M2KnowledgeableMinimum process competency
M3ResourceKSCAs according to job profile
M4TranslatorBusiness & technical needs can be integrated
M5InfluencerVision, strategy and direction can be communicated and accepted

Mastery is assessed per deliverable in three readings: Acquired (where the person is now), Required (what the activity needs) and Desired (the target they're aiming for). The gap between Acquired and Required is what improvement closes.

4 SFIA — Skills, Levels & Attributes developable axis

The skills vocabulary, and how its levels map to mastery.

SFIA (Skills Framework for the Information Age) supplies two of the four KSCA layers. Skills (121 of them, grouped by category and sub-category) each have a defined level range within 1–7. Competencies are SFIA's generic attributes — Autonomy, Influence, Complexity, Business skills, Knowledge — described at each Level of Responsibility (LOR):

LOREssence≈ Mastery
1FollowAware (M1)
2AssistKnowledgeable (M2)
3ApplyResource (M3)
4EnableTranslator (M4)
5Ensure, adviseTranslator (M4)
6Initiate, influenceInfluencer (M5)
7Set strategy, inspire, mobiliseInfluencer (M5)

This mapping is the bridge between SFIA and the mastery scale: an activity's responsibility anchor (the SFIA level its competencies sit at) sets its required mastery. "Develop Data Governance Strategy" anchors at SFIA L6–7 ≈ Influencer (M5); "Engage Change Management" at L4–5 ≈ Translator (M4). The anchor also sets the floor for the activity's defining skills — a skill required by an L6 activity should not sit two levels below it.

5 Attitudes — Disposition dispositional axis

Gregorc Thinking Styles and CliftonStrengths — natural wiring, measured as fit.

Attitudes are how a person is wired, from two instruments. Gregorc Thinking Styles describe how someone perceives and organises information — four styles, each playing a team role:

CSConcrete Sequential — the Planner ASAbstract Sequential — the Analyst CRConcrete Random — the Innovator ARAbstract Random — the Connector

Each style carries sub-elements: what the learner likes, when they learn best, and what they find hard. CliftonStrengths gives 34 themes, each rolling up to one of four domains: Strategic Thinking, Executing, Influencing, Relationship Building. A person leads with ~5 dominant themes of the 34; these are stable, not acquired.

Because both instruments measure disposition, they are read as fit — how naturally suited someone is to a kind of work — never as mastery. And because no one is all four styles at once, an activity's attitude profile is a description of the spread the team needs (§9), not a checklist for one person.

6 The Two Axes the core distinction

Developable vs dispositional; individual vs team.

Mastery (depth) — developable, individual. K/S/C are learned and levelled on M0–M5. The lever is development: training, experience, mentoring. A team pools them — the knowledge and skills a job needs can be spread across people.

Fit (wiring) — dispositional, team-composed. Attitudes don't move with training. The lever is composition: you can't make an Analyst into a Connector, so you assemble a team that covers both. Fit is assessed as coverage across the people doing the work.

These two axes are orthogonal: a person can be a strong fit for strategy work (disposition) yet low on mastery (not yet developed), or deeply masterful yet a weak fit. The CA&I measures them separately — fit tells you where to aim; mastery tells you how far you are from the target — and improves them by different levers. This is why the assessment has two units: the individual (K/S/C mastery + dominant disposition) and the team (cognitive coverage + pooled K/S).

7 The Evidence Model how mastery is proven

The deliverable is the evidence; scorecards turn it into a mastery reading.

Capability is not claimed, it is evidenced. The Deliverable an activity produces is the evidence of the KSCAs behind it. Each deliverable is assessed by three instruments:

Quality Scorecard — is it good work? Value Scorecard (KYV) — did it create useful change? Stakeholder Feedback — did the people it served accept it?

Together these resolve to a mastery reading for that deliverable — Acquired against Required and Desired (§3). This closes the loop: demand sets Required; the person's evidence sets Acquired; the gap drives the next improvement. Revised-Edition deliverables such as a stakeholder satisfaction survey and a data value measure feed directly into the Value and Stakeholder readings.

8 The Five Personas who holds capability

The Know Your Value career archetypes — a bridge from the model to real people.

People are navigated through five personas, each a 10-year direction with its own SFIA band, dominant thinking styles, strengths and development pathway:

PersonaSFIAFocusDisposition (lead)
Data & AI Executive6–7Enterprise strategy & governance — breadthAS + AR · Strategic / Influencing
Data & AI Thought Leader6–7Innovation & deep expertise — depthAS + CR · Strategic / Influencing
Data & AI Practice Manager4–5Team leadership; strategy → executionCS + AR · Relationship / Executing
Data & AI Professional2–5Builds solutions & analysesAS + CS + CR · Strategic / Executing
Data & AI Citizen (Steward)1–3Data use & quality within a domainCS + AR · Executing / Relationship

A persona's disposition sets its activity affinity — the kinds of best practice it naturally fits. A real person (a Learner instance) is assessed by a PDMMA (Professional Data Management Maturity Assessment), which reads their acquired mastery per deliverable and their dominant wiring, then points to the persona and direction that play to their strengths.

9 The Team capability composed

Why a KSCA requirement is a team requirement.

Because disposition can't be trained and no one person is all four Gregorc roles, a Best Practice is satisfied by a team, not an individual. The CA&I composes a team from personas/people and checks three things: do they cover the four cognitive roles (Analyst, Innovator, Connector, Planner); do they collectively reach the required mastery on each capability (pooled K/S/C); and are the needed strengths domains present. A gap is a missing role or a mastery shortfall — not a failing person. "All Analysts, no Connector" is the kind of gap this surfaces.
