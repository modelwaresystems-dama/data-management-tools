Phase 1 · Baseline

Measure where WAKAMOSO stands — without declaring a verdict

A live, two-dimensional Responsible-AI maturity assessment. The figures are an input to human judgment, never a pass/fail.

Two pillars — and why we assess both. The framework rests on two questions, asked separately because they fail separately.

Pillar A — Organizational maturity
“Is WAKAMOSO built to be responsible?”
The enabling environment — 13 sub-components of governance, risk processes, policy, semantics, leadership, AI literacy and value. You assign each a level 1–5.
Shows: that the organisation has the structures, accountability and capability to do responsible AI consistently and sustainably (King V conformance, and the capacity for value).
Pillar B — Operational maturity
“Is responsibility actually present in the systems we run?”
The system-level safeguards on the live platform — 8 risk dimensions: privacy, fairness, transparency, accountability, human oversight, reliability, societal benefit and GenAI controls. Each measure is in place or not.
Shows: that the safeguards exist in practice, in the product the community actually touches, today.
Why both are needed. Neither is sufficient alone. A strong organisation with weak operational controls is paper compliance — good intentions that never reach the system. Strong operational controls without the organisation are fragile and unaccountable — they depend on individuals and erode without governance, funding and oversight. Pillar A makes responsibility durable and accountable; Pillar B makes it real where it counts. Examining where the two diverge is what makes the result defensible.

The five levels — how you rate each organisational sub-component — score 0/25/50/75/100 on an evidence-of-achievement test:

1 · InitialAbsent / ad-hoc; no records.
2 · AssessingDefined or being assessed (draft, plan); not yet routine.
3 · DeterminedOperationalised & performed consistently by capable people.
4 · ManagedEffectiveness & efficiency measured (KPIs/metrics, reviewed).
5 · OptimizedImprovement goals set & pursued.

How the levels apply to Operational. You don’t pick a 1–5 level per operational measure — each is binary (in place or not). The dimension’s percentage is instead mapped onto these same band names (Initial → Optimized) on the Scorecard and Report. So the five levels label both dimensions: you set them directly on the Organisational side, while the Operational level is computed from how many applicable measures are fully operationalised.

Named Evidence + justification. The Evidence tab is the register: one named Evidence item per level and measure. Each Evidence is a collection of Artefacts — the checklist of contents you must hold — mapped to King V principles and RAI standards (NIST AI RMF, ISO 42001, EU AI Act, POPIA, FAIR/CARE). Cite the Evidence ID and confirm you hold all its Artefacts.

Report. The Report tab compiles the full results — scores, level profile with cited Evidence, Phase 2 priorities and the human sign-off — and can be printed, saved as PDF, or downloaded as a standalone HTML file.

Import / export. Export .xlsx saves a five-tab workbook (round-trips via Import). Nothing is stored online — the file is your record.

Two board-level sub-components were added in response to the King V / RAI review (flagged King V/RAI review): Board & Executive AI Literacy, and Performance & Value Creation. All other sub-components — including Policy & Standards, Semantic Governance and the GenAI dimension — are integral, supporting King V and RAI obligations directly.

WAKAMOSO Taxonomy of AI & Data Harms

Seven branches — the benefits to weigh, the harms to surface, the stakeholders affected, and where mitigation sits.

Affected Stakeholders

  • Direct participants
  • Vulnerable subgroups
  • Collective community
  • WAKAMOSO enterprise

Intended Collective Benefits

  • Digital profiles & CVs
  • Direct income via rewards

  • Community authority to control

The One-Page AI Impact & Survey Design Checklist

Showing the blank prompts. Switch to Worked example to see them answered for an illustrative survey.

1 · Purpose, Benefit & Use Cases
Integrates: Intended Collective Benefits · Societal & Ethical Harms (CARE) · AI-function risk
Reciprocity vs extraction (CARE) — Does this collection return tangible value to the community (digital profiles, wallet rewards, localised market intelligence, NGO resources), or is it extractive data mining benefiting external parties without reciprocal value?
Largely reciprocal: participants earn wallet rewards and the community gets a live water-access map plus NGO referral. The municipality and NGOs also benefit — so the team commits to returning the map to the community, not just to external parties, to keep CARE intact.
Intended collective benefit — What specific benefit is promised, to whom, and how will the community actually see it materialise (direct income, market intelligence, authority over their own data)?
Faster repair of broken water points and evidence the community can take to the municipality; direct income via rewards.
AI function & technical risk — If a third-party LLM drafts survey analytics, what is the risk of factually incorrect (hallucinated) analytics, and how is every figure checked against source data?
The LLM drafts trend summaries only. Hallucinated figures are a real risk, so its output is decision-support: every number is recomputed from source data and nothing is auto-published.
Automation / anchoring bias — Could reviewers rubber-stamp the AI’s preliminary output instead of forming an independent judgement first?
Reviewers must annotate the raw data independently before they are shown the LLM summary, to avoid rubber-stamping it.

2 · Stakeholder Impact & Community Considerations
Integrates: Affected Stakeholders · Technical & Algorithmic Harms
Stakeholder mapping — Who exactly is impacted — direct WhatsApp participants, vulnerable subgroups who may be excluded, local leaders, the collective community, and WAKAMOSO itself?
Direct WhatsApp participants; vulnerable subgroups — households without a smartphone or data, women and the elderly; the local water committee; the municipality; WAKAMOSO.
Bias & exclusion — Could specific demographics be under-represented, skewing how governments or businesses then direct resources?
A smartphone-only sample under-represents the poorest households, which could make the municipality mis-direct repairs. \u2691 coverage-bias \u2192 register
Cultural safety & misuse — Is the phrasing respectful of local norms, or could it impose/misrepresent? Could the design trigger reward-gaming (false anomalies to earn incentives) or data poisoning?
Phrasing reviewed with the local water committee. Wallet rewards create a reward-gaming risk (false “broken” reports to earn incentives) and possible data poisoning. \u2691 reward-gaming \u2192 register

3 · Consent, Data Minimisation & Privacy
Integrates: Privacy & Data Governance (POPIA)
Geotagging & re-identification — WAKAMOSO collects high-resolution geotagged data — is it adequately anonymised or aggregated before sharing so bad actors cannot re-identify vulnerable individuals?
Standpipe coordinates can co-locate to individual dwellings, so raw geotags could re-identify vulnerable households. \u2691 HIGH-RISK \u2192 EGB escalation
The consent “illusion” & authority to control — Are information sheets in plain, local languages so communities genuinely retain authority to control — or dense legalese creating only an illusion of informed consent?
Information sheet provided in isiZulu and English, plain language, explaining data use, sharing and reward terms, with a verbal walk-through by the field team.
Data minimisation — Are we collecting only the data the stated purpose actually needs?
Collect only water-point status, coordinates and a photo — no names or ID numbers.
GenAI & cross-border leaks — If community data is processed through a third-party LLM, what safeguards prevent confidentiality breaches or cross-border POPIA violations?
The LLM receives only de-identified, aggregated trend text — never raw coordinates or PII. Processor location and POPIA cross-border terms still to be confirmed. \u2691 open action \u2192 DPO

4 · Accountability & Actionable Mitigation
Integrates: Governance & Operational Harms · Outcome trade-offs
Trade-off evaluation — Do the intended collective benefits outweigh the identified risks for this specific project — and who made that call?
Collective benefit (faster water-fault response, evidence for the municipality) is judged to outweigh the risks — but only once the re-identification and coverage-bias mitigations are in place.
Fiduciary & operational accountability — Does this involve high-risk data requiring escalation to the Ethical Governance Board to prevent fiduciary blind spots?
Geotag re-identification is high-risk data, so it is escalated to the Ethical Governance Board.
Actionable mitigation — today — What specific engineering or design changes are required now, before fieldwork or model development, and who owns each?
  1. Aggregate geotags to ~100 m grid before any external sharing; never share raw participant coordinates. [Data Engineering]
  2. Verification gate — reward paid only on photo + geotag-corroborated status; de-duplicate to resist data poisoning. [Product]
  3. Coverage-gap caveat on every output (smartphone-only sample). [Analytics]
  4. Confirm LLM processor location and POPIA cross-border terms. [DPO]
  5. Independent reviewer annotates raw data before seeing the LLM summary. [Analytics lead]

The operational rule. Formatting the taxonomy as challenging questions — “Could the geotags expose participants?”, “Does this return value to them?” — turns a static risk list into an active governance tool. The prompts force the Ethics Review Working Group or a Community Advisory Panel to debate and mitigate WAKAMOSO’s unique risks while the survey is still easy to change.
